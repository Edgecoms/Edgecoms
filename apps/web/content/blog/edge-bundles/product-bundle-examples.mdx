Open your orders, filter to the last hundred, and count the ones with a single line item. On most stores that count is the biggest group on the page. You paid full price to acquire every one of those customers and they left with one thing.

The usual response is to discount something and hope. A better response is to give the shopper a reason to take two items that has nothing to do with price. That is what the eleven **product bundle examples** below are for. Each one is grouped by the motivation it serves, because the motivation is what makes it convert, not the mechanic underneath it.

Copy the construction, swap in your own catalogue, and skip the ones whose motivation your shopper does not have.

## Which starter-kit bundles work on a first-time buyer?

A first-time buyer's real problem is not price. It is that they do not know which of your products go together, and getting it wrong feels expensive. A starter kit answers that question before they have to ask it.

**1. The complete beginner set.** Everything needed to use the category once, correctly, with nothing missing. A coffee brand sells the grinder, the filter and the first bag together. A skincare brand sells cleanser, treatment and moisturiser in the order they are applied. The reason it converts is that it removes the fear of buying half a solution and discovering the gap at home. Name it for the outcome, not the contents: "Everything for your first pour-over" beats "3-piece coffee set".

**2. The trial-size sampler.** The same products in smaller sizes, priced so that trying is not a commitment. This one works where the full-size price is the barrier and the product is genuinely subjective, like fragrance, coffee, tea, or supplements. Two details matter:

- **Make the credit explicit.** Tell the buyer what happens next, and if the sampler cost comes off a later full-size order, say so on the product page rather than in an email they may not open.
- **Keep the sample count low.** A sampler with a dozen things in it becomes homework. Three or four is a tasting; twelve is a chore.

Both of these are the most direct fix for a store full of one-line orders. They intervene at the moment the shopper is least sure what else to add.

## Which refill and replenishment bundles bring people back?

Consumables have a rhythm. The bundle's job is to match it, so that the buyer stops making the decision each time.

**3. The multi-month supply.** Three or six units of the same consumable, sold as one purchase, sized to a real interval rather than a round number. If your product lasts about six weeks, sell two and call it a quarter's supply. The reason it converts is that running out is a genuine annoyance the shopper has already experienced. You are selling relief from that, and the saving is secondary.

**4. The device plus consumable pairing.** The durable item and its first supply of the thing that runs out, sold together. Handle and blades. Diffuser and oil. Frame and filters. This one has a second job beyond the first order: it teaches the customer, on day one, that the consumable is bought from you. Get that wrong and the refill purchase goes to a marketplace at a lower price forever.

<Callout title="One habit is worth more than one order" tone="warning">
Refill bundles are the only group here where a deeper discount usually pays for itself, because you are buying a repeat purchase rather than a bigger one. Decide the number against the lifetime of the customer, not the margin on that single order.
</Callout>

## Which gifting bundles sell with no discount at all?

Gift buyers are solving a time problem and a taste problem. They are not solving a price problem. This is the group where a discount most often makes the offer worse, because a cheap-looking gift is a bad gift.

**5. The ready-to-give set.** Curated, boxed, and presented as finished. The shopper is buying the certainty that it will look like a real present when it is opened. Photograph the packaging, not the products laid out flat, because the packaging is the product here. Include the card if you have one.

**6. The build-a-box at a fixed price.** The shopper chooses three items from a shortlist and the box arrives assembled. This one converts because it solves the taste problem without handing the buyer your entire catalogue. The constraints do the work:

- **Cap the shortlist.** Every item you add past six increases the chance the gift buyer abandons and buys a card instead.
- **Fix the price, not the discount.** A gift buyer arrives with a budget in mind. Matching a number they already have beats saving them an amount they did not ask for.
- **Sell the assembly.** Say explicitly that it arrives wrapped in one box. That sentence is doing more work than any percentage.

## Which build-your-own bundles suit flavours, scents and colours?

Where the difference between your products is preference rather than function, a fixed set is guessing. Let the shopper choose and you sell more units without ever being wrong.

**7. Pick any set number from a collection.** "Any 4 candles" or "any 6 sauces" at one price. It converts because the shopper is choosing between your own products instead of choosing whether to buy from you at all. The decision has been reframed from yes-or-no to which-ones.

**8. The variety pack that finds a favourite.** One of each, presented as a way to discover which one they like. The buyer gets permission to try something odd because they are also getting the safe option. Follow it up by asking which one they kept, then make the refill of that single flavour easy. This is the most reliable bridge from a variety buyer to a repeat one.

Mix-and-match is also the friendliest bundle for inventory, because the shopper picks from whatever is genuinely in stock rather than from a fixed set that breaks the moment one component sells out.

## Which accessory pairings turn one purchase into two?

The main product creates a job that is not finished at checkout. An accessory pairing finishes it.

**9. The essential accessory.** The thing the product does not work without, or works badly without. Batteries, cables, mounting hardware, the correct cleaning solution. This converts because the shopper would rather not place a second order in four days, and because being told now is a favour rather than a pitch.

**10. The protection or maintenance pairing.** The case, the cover, the conditioner, the spare part that wears first. Two rules keep this one honest:

- **Attach it to the item on screen.** A generic add-on shown against every product reads as advertising. The right accessory for the exact item reads as advice.
- **Never imply the product fails without it.** That crosses from helpful to alarming, and the return rate tells you afterwards.

## Which seasonal bundles move stock without training discount-hunters?

Seasonal bundles have an obvious risk. Run them often enough at a discount and you teach your best customers to wait. The way around it is to make the season the reason, not the price.

**11. The occasion set.** Products grouped by a use case that only exists at a particular time. Everything for a summer picnic. The winter skin routine. The first-week-of-term kit. The offer expires because the occasion does, which is true, and truth is the whole point. Under UK law, falsely stating that a product is only available for a limited time in order to force an immediate decision is a banned practice <Cite href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices">under Schedule 20 of the DMCC Act 2024</Cite>. A real occasion gives you the urgency without the invention.

Seasonal sets are also where slow stock goes to be useful. Pair the item that is not moving with the one that is, and let the season explain why they belong together.

## How do you adapt these product bundle examples to your catalogue?

Pick a motivation first, then find the products that serve it. Doing it the other way round is how stores end up with a bundle of whatever was left over.

| Motivation | Construction | Anchor item | It fails when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Uncertainty | Starter kit or sampler | Your clearest entry product | The kit contains an expert-only item |
| Habit | Multi-month or device plus refill | The consumable | The interval is a round number, not a real one |
| Time pressure | Ready-to-give or build-a-box | The packaging | You discount it and it reads as cheap |
| Taste | Pick any N, variety pack | The collection itself | The shortlist is too long to scan |
| Unfinished job | Essential or maintenance accessory | The product on screen | The accessory is generic |
| Occasion | Seasonal use-case set | The season | The deadline is invented |

Then work through it in order.

1. **Name the motivation out loud.** Write the sentence the shopper says in their head. "I do not know where to start." "I keep running out." "I need something for Saturday." If you cannot write that sentence, the bundle has no reason to exist.
2. **Choose one anchor.** Every bundle needs a single item the shopper already wanted. The rest are attached to it. Two anchors is two bundles.
3. **Cut to four items.** Then look again and see if three would do.
4. **Name it for the result.** "Winter hands rescue" tells the shopper what they get. "Bundle 2" tells them nothing.
5. **Put it where the decision happens.** Starter kits belong on the collection page and the homepage. Accessory pairings belong on the product page of the anchor. Refills belong in the post-purchase email.
6. **Run one at a time.** Two bundles competing on the same screen is a comparison, and comparisons stall.
