You just wanted something else.

This is about where a timer can live, and why that decides more than the monthly price does.

## So why do people leave?

Almost never the price. Three reasons, in the order they come up:

- **The bar is the only surface.** You want the deadline beside the buy button, in the cart drawer, on the collection grid. A bar app puts it above everything instead.
- **Sitewide is the wrong scope.** A sale on twelve products should not put a clock above the other four hundred. Page targeting helps, but you are still choosing which pages get the bar rather than which products carry a deadline.
- **The bar is the first thing people ignore.** Returning customers stop seeing it within a couple of visits. That is not a Hextom problem, it is what happens to any element pinned above the content on every page.

None of those are faults. They are the consequence of the format.

There is a fourth reason that shows up later, once a store has been running timers for a while. A bar is a single surface, so every sale competes for it. Run a shipping cutoff and a seasonal sale in the same week and one of them has to lose, or they alternate and neither registers. Stores that lean on urgency regularly outgrow one surface long before they outgrow one app.

## What should you compare instead of price?

At under ten dollars a month, price is not the variable. Compare these:

| What to check | Why it decides more than cost |
| --- | --- |
| **Placement** | Product page, cart, collection, bar. This is the whole question |
| **Scope** | Per product and per collection, or sitewide only |
| **What happens at zero** | Disappears, or sits at 00:00:00 announcing that you lied |
| **Timezone handling** | Whether a deadline reads correctly for a shopper in another country |
| **Theme app block** | Whether adding it needs a developer or a toggle |
| **Recurrence** | Daily dispatch cutoffs, which are the one recurring timer worth having |

The fourth row catches people out more than any other. A sale that ends "at midnight" is four different moments across your customer base, and an app that renders your store timezone to everyone will show a shopper in Los Angeles a deadline that already passed.

The third row deserves attention too, because it is the difference between a timer that builds trust and one that quietly destroys it. When a countdown reaches zero, three things can happen. It can vanish, which is correct. It can be replaced by a message saying the offer ended, which is better still if the price genuinely reverted. Or it can sit at 00:00:00 while the buy button carries the same price it did an hour ago, which tells every visitor from that moment on that the deadline meant nothing. Ask what an app does at zero before you install it, because the answer is rarely on the listing.

A related question worth asking: what does the app do when a shopper's clock is wrong? Some render from the device time, which means a phone set five minutes fast shows a different deadline from the one you set. Rendering from the server, with the timezone resolved server-side, is the version that stays consistent.

## Which format fits which sale?

- **One sitewide sale, one end date.** A bar is correct, and Hextom's free tier does it. Do not overthink this.
- **A deadline on specific products.** You need a product page block. A bar cannot express "these twelve items end Friday" without also shouting at everyone looking at the other four hundred.
- **A daily dispatch cutoff.** This is the most underused timer in ecommerce and it needs recurrence. It is true, it resets honestly every day, and it helps the shopper rather than pressuring them.
- **Urgency in the cart.** A different surface again, and the one closest to the decision. If your cart is a dead-end page rather than a drawer, this placement does not really exist for you.
- **Scarcity rather than time.** A stock counter is a different mechanic with a different honesty problem, and it belongs on different products from your timers rather than stacked on the same page.

Worth being blunt about the case nobody sells you: most products on most stores have no deadline at all. They are stocked, they are not on sale, and nothing changes on Friday. There is no honest timer available for those, and putting one there is how a store trains its regulars to discount everything it says. Urgency is a finite resource on a storefront. Spending it on products that have no deadline leaves none for the ones that genuinely do.

## What does the free tier actually cover?

Worth spelling out, because "free plan" means very different things across this category and Hextom's is unusually generous.

The free tier runs an event-based timer with device and page targeting. In plain terms: one sale, one end date, shown on the pages and devices you choose. For the majority of stores running two or three real promotions a year, that is the entire requirement, and paying for anything is premature.

Premium earns its money in exactly two situations:

- **You need recurrence.** Daily or weekly timers, which in practice means a dispatch cutoff. This is the single most useful recurring timer there is, and it is a legitimate reason to upgrade.
- **You run several campaigns at once.** Unlimited active bars matters when a shipping cutoff and a seasonal sale need to coexist rather than take turns.

Geo and UTM targeting sound compelling and are worth less than they appear for most stores. Targeting a timer by traffic source means the same product shows different deadlines to different people, which is defensible for a genuine channel-specific promotion and indefensible otherwise.

## What does this category get wrong generally?

Three patterns show up across almost every timer app, and knowing them makes any comparison faster.

- **The default is perpetual.** Most apps make a rolling timer the easy option and a fixed deadline the configured one. That default is why so many storefronts carry a clock that resets, and why so many shoppers have learned to ignore clocks entirely.
- **Zero-state is an afterthought.** Very few listings tell you what happens when the countdown ends, because the honest answer is often "nothing, it sits there".
- **Placement is sold as a feature list rather than a decision.** Apps advertise the number of surfaces they support. What matters is whether the one surface you need is among them, and for most stores that is the product page rather than the count.

If you take one thing into any comparison, make it the zero-state question. It is the cheapest way to separate an app built by someone who thought about the shopper from one built to a feature checklist.

## What breaks when you switch?

Very little, which is the good news about this category. A timer holds no data. It is a date and a display.

1. **Note what your current timer says** and when it is due to end, so you can reproduce it.
2. **Install the new app and configure the same deadline** without removing the old one.
3. **Check both are not showing at once.** Two clocks disagreeing by a minute is worse than either alone.
4. **Remove the old app's theme app block**, not just the app. An orphaned block can leave an empty gap or a timer frozen at zero.
5. **Load the page as a logged-out visitor on a phone**, because that is where a new block is most likely to push the buy button below the fold.
6. **Check the deadline in a second timezone** before you trust it.

<Callout title="The check nobody runs" tone="warning">
Set a timer to expire in five minutes, then watch what the page does when it hits zero. If it sits at 00:00:00, every visitor after that moment is being told the offer ended and is still being asked to buy at the same price. That is worse than having no timer at all.
