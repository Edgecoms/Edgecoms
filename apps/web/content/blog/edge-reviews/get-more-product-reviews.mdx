You have 180 products. Fourteen of them have reviews. Nine of those fourteen are the same bestseller people were going to buy with or without proof.

That is a coverage problem, not a persuasion problem, and it does not get solved by rewriting a subject line. Most advice on **how to get more product reviews** stops at the request email, which is one variable inside one channel that never reaches a large share of your buyers.

This post is about the levers outside the send. Which products to target first, which buyers your ask misses entirely, what a printed insert does that an email cannot, and which shortcuts cost more than the reviews are worth.

## Which products in your catalogue are still sitting on zero?

A product on zero is the most expensive gap you have. Going from none to one changes how the page reads more than going from fifty to sixty ever will. Yet almost nobody measures coverage. They measure total review count, which is the number that hides the problem.

Run the audit:

1. Export every product with units shipped in the last 90 days.
2. Export review counts per product from your review app.
3. Join the two on product ID, not on title. Titles drift.
4. Filter to products with a review count of zero.
5. Sort by units shipped, descending. The top of that list is your queue.

The queue is ordered by demand, not catalogue position. A product on zero shipping forty units a month costs you conversions every week. One that ships a unit a quarter is not worth a campaign.

| Coverage tier | What the page looks like | What it needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zero reviews, ships well | Untested, and the gap is visible | A targeted backfill to past buyers |
| One to three reviews | Credible, thin | Normal request flow will finish the job |
| Ten or more, all old | Dated, possibly wrong | Fresh reviews, not more reviews |
| Fifty plus, recent | Done | Stop spending here |

The last row matters. Most stores keep pushing requests at the product that already has proof, because the flow is untargeted and that product ships most.

## Is your ask reaching every buyer, or only your email subscribers?

Take the orders you shipped last month, then the review requests that actually landed in an inbox. The gap is usually wider than anyone expects, and every order in it is a review you were never going to get.

- **Marketing consent.** Review requests are usually classified as marketing, not transactional. Buyers who declined at checkout are excluded before your flow runs.
- **Guest checkout typos.** A mistyped address bounces silently as far as your review app knows. The order still counts, the ask does not.
- **Gift orders.** The buyer paid. The recipient owns the product. Asking the buyer gets you nothing, or worse, a review from somebody who never opened the box.
- **Point of sale and wholesale.** Real orders, both usually sitting outside the flow entirely.
- **Suppressed repeat buyers.** Frequency caps and quiet periods on your email platform swallow the request silently.

Fix the plumbing before you fix the copy. A plain ask that reaches every buyer beats a polished one that reaches half of them.

## Does SMS collect anything email no longer reaches?

Yes, and it is worth being precise about why. SMS is not a faster email. It reaches different people: the buyer who has not opened a marketing email in two years but reads every text within minutes.

- **Consent is separate.** An email subscriber is not an SMS subscriber. You need the number and the explicit opt-in, and the post-purchase page is the cleanest place to collect both.
- **It costs per message,** so it does not belong on every order. Point it at the coverage queue above, where a single review changes the page.
- **The link has to land on a one-tap form.** A text is read on a phone, standing up. If it opens a login wall or asks for a name and email, you have spent the message for nothing.
- **One message, no chase.** SMS has no tolerance for a follow-up sequence.

Used narrowly, it works. Used as a second broadcast channel, it burns the list.

## What can a packing insert or a QR code add that a send cannot?

An insert is the only ask that reaches a buyer with no valid email, no marketing consent, and no phone number. It also arrives at the one moment the product is genuinely interesting to its owner: the box is open.

What separates an insert that works from one that goes in the recycling:

- **Point the QR code at a short, measurable URL,** not your homepage. If you cannot count scans, you cannot decide whether to reprint.
- **Land on the review form itself,** pre-filled with the product where you can. Every tap between the scan and the first star loses people.
- **Print the ask, not the brand story.** One sentence and a code.
- **Never make it conditional.** An insert that says "scan if you loved it" is review gating in printed form, and the average it produces is worthless.

The honest limit: inserts are slow to test. The batch is printed and in transit before you learn anything, so treat it as a background channel, not something you tune.

## Should the post-purchase page ask before the customer navigates away?

Partly. The post-purchase page has the highest attention of any page you own. But asking someone to review a product still in a warehouse produces a review from someone holding nothing.

Use it for the two things it is actually good for:

- **Capture SMS consent for the later ask.** The customer is already in a completing frame of mind. This is the cheapest place on the site to grow the channel above.
- **Ask about a previous order.** A repeat buyer from three weeks ago has a delivered product sitting at home. That ask is legitimate right now, and most stores never make it.

Both raise volume. Neither adds reviews written by people guessing.

## How to get more product reviews on a product that launched last week

New products are where the coverage gap starts, so plan for it before the launch rather than after.

- **Ask the buyers of the previous version.** If this is a v2, the people who owned v1 are your only qualified early reviewers. Send them the new one and ask.
- **Seed with disclosed samples.** Free units to real customers are fine when the review is labelled as gifted and the reward does not depend on the rating. Keep your hands off the words. Google says an entity that controls the reviews about itself is not eligible for review stars. <Cite href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/review-snippet">Google's review snippet guidance</Cite>
- **Shorten the delay for launch products only.** Your standard flow might wait fourteen days after delivery. For a launch, seven puts proof on the page while the traffic is still arriving.

## Can one review cover a variant family, or does every SKU need its own?

Inside Shopify, variants sit under one product, so this only bites when the same item is published as several separate products. Colourways split into their own listings are the usual culprit.

- **Group only genuinely identical items.** Same product, different colour or size run. Grouping a 250ml and a 1L bottle of the same formula is defensible. Grouping two different formulas is not.
- **Show what the reviewer actually bought.** "The strap is too short" means something different on the small than on the large. Display the purchased variant on the review.
- **Keep the review visible on every page it counts for.** Google requires that marked-up review content be readily available to users on the page carrying the markup, so a grouped review has to render on each page, not just the parent. <Cite href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/review-snippet">See Google's requirements</Cite>

Done carefully, grouping is the fastest coverage lift a store with a wide colour range has. Done carelessly it misleads, and one complaint costs more than the reviews gained.

## How do you keep the corpus current rather than merely large?

Volume decays. Sixty reviews, none written in two years, describe a version of the product that may no longer exist. Recency is part of whether reviews work.

- **Re-ask on repeat purchase.** Someone buying a consumable for the fourth time is your best source of a current review, and almost no flow asks.
- **Reset expectations after a formulation or supplier change.** The old reviews describe the old product. Keep them, date them clearly, and prioritise fresh collection.
- **Sort so recent reviews surface,** and always show the date. A hidden date reads as something to hide.

<Example>
Say you ship 900 orders a month and the ask reaches 70% of them: 630 requests. If 4% produce a review, that is roughly 25 new reviews a month. Spread untargeted across 180 products, that is one review per product every seven months. Pointed at the 20 products on zero, the same 25 reviews clear the queue inside a month. Substitute your own numbers.
</Example>

The arithmetic is why targeting beats broadcasting. The total is the same. The result is not.

## Which shortcuts to volume cost more than the reviews are worth?

- **Buying reviews.** The fastest way to lose the trust the reviews were there to build, and obvious to anyone who scans four in a row and notices they read the same. We went through how buyers spot them in [our piece on fake reviews](/blog/fake-reviews-shopify).
- **Gating on sentiment.** Asking how the customer feels first, then routing only the happy ones to the form. It inflates your average, it is dishonest, and platforms treat it as manipulation.
- **Aggregating from other websites.** Google's review markup covers reviews on your own page, not reviews scraped from elsewhere. Migrating reviews you already own from a previous app is different, and legitimate.
- **Rewarding only positive reviews.** Any incentive must go to everyone who reviews, whatever the rating, and must be disclosed.
- **Deleting the negatives.** A wall of five stars reads as curated. The critical reviews make the rest believable.

Every one buys a number. None buys the thing the number was supposed to represent. Our [complete guide to Shopify product reviews](/blog/shopify-product-reviews) covers the display and markup side.
